About this project

A map of people connected to discipline-based education research (DBER) — physics, engineering, biology, chemistry, math, and general STEM education research — built from public sources and merged into one dataset.

What you're looking at

The network view shows every person in the dataset as a small dot. Pick an attribute in the sidebar — Institution, Program, DBER Field, or PhD Era — and people who share that attribute get pulled toward a shared "hub" node and colored together. Click a hub to isolate its members; click a person to see everything the dataset has on them.

Read this before you read the graph as a social network. The lines connecting people are shared-attribute connections — same institution, same program, same field — not confirmed real-world relationships. Two people linked to the same "Purdue University" hub may never have met. The clustering is genuinely informative about who's affiliated with what, but it isn't a map of who knows whom, who advised whom, or who has worked together.

You'll also notice that grouping by Institution and by Program often produce nearly identical clusters for the largest schools — Purdue's School of Engineering Education essentially is Purdue's presence in this dataset. That's not a bug; it reflects how the underlying data was collected (see below), which leans heavily on institutions that happen to publish a complete, dated alumni roster.

How the dataset was built

This started as a side project studying postdoctoral job markets in physics education research (PER), on the premise that engineering, science, and mathematics education research all belong to the same broader family of discipline-based education research fields. That grew into an effort to map who actually works in this space — current faculty, PhD program alumni, and recognized field leaders — by merging nine separate source lists (a tenth, job-posting and institution-list files with no named people in them, contributed nothing and was excluded):

  1. A STEM DBER Alliance member list (name, institution, field, position)
  2. A 2026 Upstate NY DBER conference attendee directory
  3. An index of profiles from an Engineering Education Pioneers project
  4. A compiled report of named PhD alumni across roughly 30 US engineering, science, and mathematics education doctoral programs
  5. Follow-up research filling in gaps for programs with no public alumni roster, using institutional repositories and ProQuest-style dissertation records
  6. A current-faculty research pass across the same ~30 programs
  7. A cross-reference against ASEE's Educational Research and Methods (ERM) Division Fellows list and the general Academy of Fellows, 2023–2025 classes
  8. An international research pass covering Canada, Scandinavia, and Australia
  9. Manual review to reconcile the inevitable near-duplicates — nicknames, dropped middle initials, and punctuation differences that made the same person look like two people across sources

The dataset has grown substantially since that original merge, mostly through the co-author mining described next — see the live count at the bottom of this page for today's total.

The August 2026 expansion: coauthor-network mining

Starting in August 2026, the dataset began growing through a different method: scanning the published papers of people already in the dataset for their frequent co-authors. Someone who repeatedly co-authors DBER-relevant work alongside a scholar already confirmed to work in this space gets added as a new entry. The first pass surfaced roughly 3,400 people this way — collaborators, co-PIs, and colleagues who don't show up on any alumni roster or conference attendee list, and who the original nine sources above had no way of finding.

These entries are algorithmically surfaced, not hand-vetted. Co-authoring a paper with a DBER scholar is a weaker signal than the sources above — it can just as easily mean a one-off collaboration outside someone's main field, a shared methods paper, or a mismatched name. A follow-up pass re-checks each one against a Google Scholar identity match where one can be found — comparing name, institution, and whether their publications actually appear in DBER-relevant venues — but that's still an automated check, not a human one. This site only publishes citation counts, h-index, and match confidence for people matched via Google Scholar; candidates whose only available identity match came from Semantic Scholar are left without those fields rather than shown with data from a different source. None of these outcomes get a person removed automatically — every record stays flagged for human review either way. If you spot one that doesn't belong, or a connection that's wrong, flag it via the Add/Edit page.

DBER field inference (August 2026)

Most people in this dataset have never had a DBER field (PER, EER, BER, RUME, etc.) recorded at all — it was only ever entered when a source explicitly stated it. To close that gap, a keyword-and-venue classifier was run over the text already in the dataset for anyone without a field tag: research interests, dissertation titles, and the journals/conferences their publications appeared in. A match to a clearly DBER-specific venue (e.g. the Journal of Engineering Education, an ASEE conference proceedings, CBE—Life Sciences Education) counts as stronger evidence than a bare keyword match in free text.

These are suggestions, not confirmed field tags. A machine-inferred field is shown as a dashed, separate chip from any hand-sourced DBER Field tag — never merged into it — and hovering it shows the matched evidence and a rough confidence level. It only fills in where a person had no tag at all; it never overrides or second-guesses a tag that was already sourced from the original lists. Think of it as a lead worth a second look, not a verified classification — correct it via the Add/Edit page if it's wrong.

Identity verification — Google Scholar only

Every scholar record here that carries a name gets checked against a live Google Scholar profile where one can be found (using Google web search plus Google Scholar's own author-search API), so citation counts and h-index (shown in the network view as node size, and in each person's detail panel) come from a real match, not a guess. That match is scored a confidence level — high, medium, or low — based on whether the name, institution, and the profile's actual research topics line up. The live breakdown for what's on this site right now is in the stats table below — a fair share of matches are medium- or low-confidence, which means the citation count attached to some people may belong to someone else with a similar name. The detail panel flags this with a dotted underline on "Citations"/"h-index" wherever the match isn't high-confidence.

This site publishes Google Scholar matches only — never Semantic Scholar — and drops weak Google Scholar matches too. The separately maintained master dataset this site draws from checks some coauthor-mined candidates' identities against Semantic Scholar as well as Google Scholar; anyone whose only available match came from Semantic Scholar shows up here with no citation count, h-index, or match confidence at all. On top of that, a same-name Google Scholar match where neither the person's institution nor their research field lined up with anything already on file gets dropped too, regardless of what confidence level the matching pass assigned it — checked against real examples site visitors flagged as clearly the wrong person. In every case the field is simply left blank rather than shown from an unreliable source. The "none" count in the match-confidence breakdown below covers all of these: no Scholar match at all, a Semantic-Scholar-only match, or a Google Scholar match too weak to trust.

Known gaps and limitations

  • Coverage is uneven across fields and institutions. Programs that publish a well-maintained public alumni page (Purdue, Virginia Tech, Western Michigan, San Diego State/UC San Diego, Maryland, Delaware, and a handful of others) are disproportionately well-represented — not because they're larger or more active, just more publicly documented.
  • Most fields are sparsely filled. A hand-sourced field tag (PER, EER, BER, RUME, etc.) is only recorded for a minority of people; research interests, dissertation titles, and current position are similarly partial. The machine-inferred field layer (see above) fills in many — not all — of the gaps, and is clearly marked as inferred rather than sourced. Where a record still looks thin, that reflects a genuine gap in the public record, not a collection error.
  • ASEE's historical Fellows roster is incomplete. It sits behind a members-only login; only the 2023–2025 classes were retrievable in full. The more directly DBER-relevant ERM Division Fellows list was retrieved in full.
  • No names were invented. Where a source didn't clearly confirm a detail, that field was left blank rather than guessed.
  • A large share of the dataset is unreviewed coauthor-mining output. Most people added since August 2026 were surfaced by scanning existing scholars' publication co-author lists (see above), not hand-curated from the original nine sources. These entries haven't been individually verified as DBER scholars — treat them as leads worth a second look, not confirmed identities.
  • Email addresses are deliberately excluded from this public site, even where a source (a conference directory) originally included them — republishing scraped contact information without the person's direct consent isn't something this project does. The same rule applies to every dataset merged in since.
  • The "Show collaboration signals" lines on the network page are the least verified data here. They show who a co-author-mined person was found via — a real signal, but an algorithmic one, not a confirmed collaboration or advising relationship. They're off by default for that reason.
